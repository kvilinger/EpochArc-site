Here’s a quick breakdown of what the three new results are and why they matter.

## **Kakeya**

The Kakeya conjecture is about swirling a needle or stylus around in the air cleverly—so that it points in every direction once but sweeps out as little volume as possible. Specifically, the conjecture says that the volume cannot be less than a certain number. Surprisingly, this simple geometry problem is one of the most important conjectures in harmonic analysis, the study of how all kinds of mathematical objects can be built from waves.

Last year mathematicians Hong Wang and Joshua Zahl [proved the conjecture in three dimensions](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/the-kakeya-conjecture-a-decades-old-math-problem-is-solved-in-three/) in one of the biggest math breakthroughs of this century. The feat won Wang [a Fields Medal](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/2026-fields-medals-go-to-four-young-mathematicians/)—math’s highest honor—at this summer’s International Congress of Mathematicians in Philadelphia. But the problem remained open in higher-dimensional spaces.

In last month’s “[slop drop](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/mathematicians-marvel-and-grumble-at-openais-trove-of-new-results/),” OpenAI’s internal model [solved the four-dimensional Kakeya problem](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/the-most-exciting-claims-from-openais-heap-of-new-proofs/). Now it’s solved the problem in _every_ dimension, proving that there’s a limit to how small a space a high-dimensional stylus can take up.
