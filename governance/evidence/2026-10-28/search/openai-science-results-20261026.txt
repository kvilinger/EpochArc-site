### Where the OpenAI discoveries sit in the wider release

These results follow OpenAI’s 6 October release of 722 mathematics manuscripts, which we covered in [OpenAI Just Posted Hundreds More Results on Major Math Problems](https://www.progressiverobot.com/2026/10/07/openai-math-results-hundreds-more-major-problems/). ### Why this batch is different

Earlier releases were document drops, which drew sharp criticism from mathematicians, as we reported in [OpenAI Keeps Bulldozing Mathematicians](https://www.progressiverobot.com/2026/09/28/math-community-openai-keeps-bulldozing-mathematicians/). OpenAI says it “reached out to external experts and asked them to act as shepherds, organizing groups to understand the new results.” Each shepherd picked a different way to handle the material.

### Mathematics: release the raw output

For the local smoothing result, the expert advised OpenAI to “release the AI output, leaving researchers free to organize their own efforts to explain the result.” This copies the approach of the 6 October release, and it makes this the most open of the OpenAI discoveries. Anyone can download the paper and the Lean files today.

### Computer science: an expert group, with everything public

For the Exponential Time Hypothesis, OpenAI formed an external group of collaborators “while making the AI output publicly available for anyone to examine and build on.” The group asked for the raw data to go public immediately, and OpenAI agreed.

### Physics: the expert group controls the release

For the quantum field theory result, the group “decided to hold off on releasing the AI-produced artifacts and instead work on writing it up themselves.” So of the three OpenAI discoveries, this is the only one that outsiders cannot yet read.

## Local Smoothing and the Kakeya Conjecture

In 1991, Sogge proposed the local smoothing conjecture, which concerns how waves behave as they become focused and holds that the most extreme focusing can only be short-lived, so that focusing at a typical time is far weaker than at the worst-case time. The announcement described the conjectured phenomenon as fundamental, with wide-ranging implications throughout mathematical analysis; the conjecture implies the Kakeya conjecture and Stein’s restriction conjecture. Guth, Wang, and Zhang proved the conjecture for two spatial dimensions in 2019, and Wang and Zahl settled the three-dimensional Kakeya conjecture in 2025.

OpenAI said its models produced a proof of the local smoothing conjecture in every dimension, relying on an auxiliary partial differential equation of a novel kind, and in doing so also resolved the Kakeya conjecture and Stein’s restriction conjecture in all dimensions. The company obtained the AI output on October 18, 2026, formalized it in Lean the following day, and then contacted an expert to discuss forming a collaboration around the result. Following that expert’s recommendation, OpenAI published both an AI natural-language writeup produced by its models and a Lean proof in its public GitHub repository, the same strategy it used for its October 6, 2026 mathematics release.

### **Computer Science: Is ETH Overthrown?**

OpenAI stated that its system demonstrated that the Exponential Time Hypothesis (ETH) is false, a result subsequently verified in Lean.

Here, ETH refers to the Exponential Time Hypothesis in computer science, not Ethereum.

ETH (Exponential Time Hypothesis) is a "pessimistic conjecture" in computer science: for a certain class of logical problems, no matter how algorithms are improved, the worst-case runtime will grow exponentially with problem size, with no true shortcuts.

Thus, the overturning of ETH has reopened the question of how much faster these tasks can become, and may also point the way toward better algorithms. OpenAI also noted that substantial follow-up research is still needed to find such algorithms and translate theoretical advances into practical improvements.

For readers in the crypto space, a more direct question is: Will this affect existing cryptographic systems? The security of many cryptographic schemes relies on the difficulty of solving certain specific computational problems. Professor Ryan Williams from MIT asked OpenAI to assess the potential impact. According to his account, OpenAI has conducted the assessment and is reasonably confident that this result does not imply existing cryptographic systems have been broken.

### **Physics: Laying the Foundation for Quantum Field Theory**

Physicists use quantum field theory to describe the world of particles with extremely accurate predictions, but its mathematical foundation is not rigorous.

OpenAI stated that the model provides rigorous "existence" proofs for pure Yang–Mills theory, quantum chromodynamics (QCD—the theory describing the strong force between quarks and gluons), and a grand unified theory incorporating the Standard Model gauge fields. ### **Mathematics: Wave focusing and the "needle placement" problem**

OpenAI stated that its models have proven the local smoothness conjecture in all dimensions, thereby solving the Kakeya conjecture and the Stein restriction conjecture in all dimensions.

The local smoothing conjecture, proposed by Sogge in 1991, studies the behavior of waves when they focus. A helpful analogy is water waves: several ripples moving toward the same point may momentarily combine to form a very high crest. However, how intense this instant is does not directly reflect the average behavior over the entire time period.

The local smoothing conjecture requires precise mathematical estimates to describe the relationship between these instantaneous extreme behaviors and time averages. Due to its fundamental significance, proving it would also impact other mathematical problems, including the Kakeya conjecture and the Stein restriction conjecture.

The Kakeya conjecture can be understood through the "needle placement" problem: imagine placing many needles of the same length and no thickness in space, such that there is at least one needle in every possible direction. These needles can be translated and overlap with each other. What is the smallest possible size of the set formed by these needles?

The key to measuring "size" here is the mathematical concept of dimension. In three-dimensional space, the Kakeya conjecture holds that such a set must have full three-dimensional measure; in higher-dimensional spaces, it must similarly have the corresponding full dimension. Dimension is a distinct concept from volume, so it should not be simply understood as how many cubic meters these needles occupy.

Previously, the two-dimensional local smoothness (2019) and the three-dimensional Kakeya conjecture (2025, by Wang and Zahl) had been proven separately.
