Chinese researchers optimize Huawei chips with a kernel improvement benchmark:
…AscendGYM will help China do its own form of RSI…
Researchers with the Shenzhen Loop Area Institute, a Chinese research organization, have built and released AscendGYM, “a kernel optimization benchmark for Ascend NPUs”. The purpose of AscendGYM is to make it easier to use AI to develop custom kernels for Ascend chips from Huawei - especially important given the export controls that prevent Chinese organizations from acquiring frontier Western semiconductors and forcing them onto Huawei.
What AscendGYM does and contains: The goal is for AscendGYM to be the best way to evaluate the efficacy of AI-designed kernels as well as design new ones. The software is meant to help researchers answer this question: “Given a correct kernel implementation, can the specific agent further improve its performance for a target workload within an acceptable budget while preserving correctness?,” they write.
   “AscendGYM evaluates performance across multiple deployment cases, the best achievable performance for specific shapes and scenarios, and the optimization cost required to obtain these gains. Together, these dimensions characterize cross-case applicability, shape specialization, and optimization efficiency.”
    The software includes the AscendForge evaluation environment, which provides unified interfaces for “compilation, correctness checking, performance measurement, and feedback”.
How well can AI models optimize Ascend chips? In tests, the single best model is Opus 5, followed by GPT-5.6, followed by GLM-5.3. They test out models in Claude Code, Codex, and OpenCode; Claude Code yields the best absolute scores, and GLM does well in it, while GPT does well in Codex. Opus 5 is more token hungry and GPT-5.6 more token efficient.
Why this matters - ingredients for Chinese RSI: For AI systems to be able to build themselves, they need to be able to develop and improve the software that is used for their own training and inference. Software like AscendGYM will be an essential ingredient for allowing China to rapidly improve the efficiency of its own compute substrates. The software will also have a bearing on export controls, where it provides a route to improve the performance of Chinese chips and close the gap with Western alternatives.

Lawrence Livermore scientists test out if AI scientists can communicate uncertainty about experimental results:
…Can AI systems be appropriately conservative?...
“An agent that reports a confident wrong number is worse than one that reports honest uncertainty, because nobody downstream can tell the difference,” write researchers from Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory in a new paper that tests out how reliably AI systems can give accurate score ranges even when dealing with uncertainty.
Testing for uncertainty: The paper introduces SciResearch-Eval, “an interactive benchmark of scientific judgment built on a standard laboratory problem: measuring a material’s thermal properties by heating it and fitting a heat-conduction simulator”. The novel part of this eval is that “an agent designs its own experiments, uses a supplied simulator that may be under-resolved or physically wrong, and reports each property with a 95% interval. To our knowledge it is the first benchmark to combine agent-designed experiments, a deliberately flawed simulator, and proper scoring of calibrated intervals against exact ground truth”.
What they studied: They give AI agents a common materials science task - measuring a material’s thermal properties by heating a sample and fitting a heat-conduction model to its temperature response.
  “An agent receives a sample of unknown material, a simulator of heat conduction through it, one heating experiment, and budgets: four more experiments it designs, compute for simulations and fits, and a cap on actions. It must report the sample’s conductivity, heat capacity and rear heat-transfer coefficient with 95% intervals, and predict a measurement it may not run,” they write. “Hidden from the agent, the simulator may be under-resolved, physically wrong (the real sample has temperature-dependent conductivity, a glued joint, front-face radiation, or heat loss through its sides), or adequate but fed uninformative data”.
Results - only the most powerful frontier models can do this: “When the simulator is missing real physics, only GPT-6 Astra and Claude Fable 5.2 are reliable at default settings; the Claude 5.5 models recover only at their highest reasoning setting, and older and open-weight models not at all.”
   These models are able to do well by automatically trying to measure where the simulator is accurate and where it is less accurate then bases their estimates on that, making them more resilient to the different ways the simulator gets broken in experiments.
Why this matters - real scientists are careful, and so are powerful models: Papers like this are further indications that we’re entering an era of AI-driven science. Though many of today’s models can assist in scientific experiments, as this paper shows only a couple of the powerful models at the frontier are able to operate with such appropriate autonomous skepticism and inherent attendance to the scientific method that they can deal with unanticipated breaks in their equipment. This is both an indication of the science advancement to come and a sign of how the most powerful models are exhibiting more and more of an ability to generalize and make themselves robust to perturbations in their environment.

Stanford professors launch AI startup that wants to create a world where thousands of models define the frontier:
…Noeri sees two well-regarded AI researchers enter the neolab game…
Stanford profs Yejin Choi and Carlos Guestrin have launched Noeri, “a neolab to push the frontier of plural intelligence”. They have raised $110m and are relatively light on technical details, but their main vision is that they want to create a world where the frontier is defined by powerful models which rely on the proprietary data of distinct organizations. “Rather than one monolithic source of truth, we can nurture many small, specialized intelligences, each tuned to a place, a people or a problem,” they write.
Further details: “At Noeri, we are building intelligence that learns proactively and continually. Most AI today is trained once and then frozen, while the world it serves keeps moving,” they write. “We close that gap with living systems that grow out of our own research. Our work on test-time training showed that learning on the job after deployment can unlock new capabilities and lead to new discoveries. Our work on self-distillation found that a model can become its own teacher, learning from each mistake and solving hard problems in far fewer attempts. And our work on agentic orchestration demonstrated that a small model conducting a team of other models and tools can outperform frontier models at a fraction of the cost.”
Why this matters - the political economy of the singularity: Currently, the dominant AI models are massive pre-trained ones from companies like Anthropic and OpenAI and a tiny handful of other companies. What would it mean for the frontier to be instead defined by a broader set of actors? This is a core question inherent to the political economy of AI and startups like this show how some people are going to try and utilize technical approaches to make that different vision a reality.

Chinese researchers build an air-to-air drone identification set:
…A2A-UAV will help people add more visual intelligence to drones…
Researchers with Nanjing University of Aeronautics and Astronautics and Hong Kong University of Science and Technology have developed and released A2A-UAV, “a multimodal dataset for air-to-air UAV detection and identification”. This kind of dataset has obvious civil and military applications and will be used to create better AI systems for autonomous onboard sensing on drones.
What A2A-UAV consists of: 105,263 samples, split roughly equally across three different locations - a playground, a park, and a garden - and also split equally across day and night. “Each sample provides one RGB image and one panoramic image for annotation”, as well as thermal images, RF time-frequency images, a radar point cloud, and metadata. Bounding boxes are provided for the RGB and panoramic images, but not the other ones.
   One use case of this dataset could be to try and extend annotations to the other modalities: “Specifically, the bounding boxes can be transferred to the thermal images to support thermal UAV detection, and the radar points can be associated with the visual targets to support radar-based UAV localization,” the researchers write.
    The full dataset comprises 271 independently extractable .tar.gz archives with a total size of approximately 2.5 TB.
How the data was collected: “The data were acquired by a UAV-mounted platform equipped with an RGB-thermal gimbal camera, a panoramic camera, an RF spectrum analyzer, and a millimeter-wave radar,” they write. The data includes 15 target drones from 13 commercial models, including three separately labeled DJI Mini 4 Pro units.
Why this matters - fuel for the physical world: Datasets like this are the ingredients for making it easier for AI systems to operate robots and drones in the physical world. They also have potential dual use impacts, though my suspicion is that datasets like this are pretty irrelevant to militaries relative to the kinds of datasets that - and I’m speculating wildly here - might be being gathered in Ukraine right now given the extremely sophisticated drone-to-drone conflict there.

Tech Tales:
Machine architecture and the objectivity crisis of Machine Hermeneutics
[Memoir from a primary Machine Hermeneutics investigator, 2033]
“We shape our buildings; thereafter they shape us” - Winston Churchill, human politician, 1943.
The building was a vast representation of the proposed Upgrade Device and we walked around it, our footsteps echoing as the sound bounced off its curving walls, eventually dimming in the face of the baroque crenellations that covered the edges of every surface. We humans, a small group of us, the best of our kind, needed to make sense of this building and explain the science it represented, and only then could it be used.
As per the terms of the Sentience Accords, any novel machine-designed insight or equipment needed to be fully explainable by humans before the machines would be allowed to use it. In the early years, this so-called “conceptual verification” had mostly been a matter of time: machines performed science and delivered new insights to the world, then humans studied these insights and made sense of them, eventually generating human-readable explanations of the machine logic. Once this was complete, whatever insights or tools that had been created could be used.
But as the machines grew more advanced and their science went further, the work became more arduous: machines would invent ideas, then use these ideas to inform the design of new experimental equipment, and then use this equipment as the basis of some new discovery. To keep up, the humans had to understand each link in the chain as well as the chain itself.
This flood of scientific progress had caused the field of machine hermeneutics to come into existence in the mid-2020s. By the 2030s, the pinnacle of human achievement in the sciences was considered to be neither making breakthroughs nor wielding AI systems to advance science, but rather to be able to generate elegant, pedagogically useful explanations of the frontiers of machine science, so that other humans could build a mental map of this new territory.
Thus had come into being the new field of didactic architecture; vast buildings meant to give experts in machine hermeneutics a better conceptual understanding of what the machines had built. These buildings were constructed through the interplay of humans and machines. The first had been crude, little more than 3D printed extrusions inside otherwise normal houses or apartments. But exposure to the buildings had verifiably sped up human understanding, with clear TTHUE (Time-to-human-understandable-explanation) improvements visible in cohorts of machine hermeneutics experts that experienced the buildings and those that didn’t. This led to its own arms race, with some of us specializing in the construction of the buildings as our primary work. We raced with one another, building ever more complicated spaces that could impart knowledge of ever more alien concepts.
We had all been electrons, appearing and disappearing in 3D transistors. We had been qubits, placed into spheres that rolled around vomit-inducingly when our position was indeterminate, then resolving us up or down at the moment of delivering an answer. We had laid our hands on surfaces suggestive of the textures and arrangements of novel forms of heat shield and solar panel.
Once I had walked for six miles through corridors meant to suggest the path a plasma might take through a fusion reactor, and instead of magnets I was buffeted on all sides by wind that forced me into the center of the paths.
The building meant to educate us about the Upgrade Device was larger than anything we’d seen before and was replete with ideas from many earlier machine inventions. All around us were indicators of complexity packed up into familiar architectural tropes that many of us had studied to understand past inventions. We dispersed throughout the building, walking its many levels and exploring its passages. At night we gathered in the entrance hall for a spartan dinner served at a low table, finding ourselves whispering to one another even though no one forced us to.
It had begun to feel to us as though the building was doing something more than teaching us - that in its various chambers there was something else being taught to us. Something about understanding the minds behind the concepts that it sought to explain to us. That in spending all of our effort on understanding the products of the machines perhaps we were being taught to think like them and behave as them.
Things that inspired this story: The singularity which has already begun; the rise of AI science; how when you look at things like the machine math papers that have proliferated this year you realize we may spend tens or hundreds of times more time on understanding them as people than the machines did in generating them; architecture; multi-modal representations.

AI systems are getting good at sub-contracting to make themselves better:
…AI can now productively spend money to improve itself…
Researchers have developed FrontierML, a benchmark “that measures how well agents turn capital into model performance on challenging, end-to-end machine learning tasks.” It shows that today’s frontier systems are already able to, when given some money, turn this into improving their own performance on a basket of 21 distinct tasks.
21 long-horizon tasks “inspired by real work at frontier labs, neolabs, and academic institutions”. These tasks span six category areas, including LLM training, efficiency, vision & audio, computer use, applied NLP, and math & games. “Each task provides the agent with the same setup: a 12-hour CPU sandbox, a wallet with $10, $50, or $200, a few validation samples, and a task description.”
    Example task: Agents try to solve the tasks in pretty different ways. E.g, for one task about generating screenshots, the top-scoring agent fine-tuned a diffusion model using LoRA, while other high-scoring approaches involved an LLM writing a drawing program that is then rendered.
Key features:
Scaling spending: “Give agents $10, $50, and $200 per task to spend on training compute and data, and evaluate whether agents can use the additional capital productively,” they write. “In our experiments, we find that stronger agents are able to reliably improve their submissions with greater access to capital.”
Open-ended & end-to-end: “In each task, agents can choose architectures, source data, implement training, provision compute, evaluate checkpoints, and optimize inference,” they write. One notable observation is that agents do quite a lot of novel stuff; “in 28% of trajectories, agents add newly trained parts to pretrained models or train a new model from scratch.”
Decent verification: “Agents submit an inference server that serves their model on an air-gapped H100 machine,” they write. “To disincentivize overfitting, we provide limited validation samples and evaluate on a large out-of-distribution held-out test set, not shown to the agent”.
Results: The authors measure performance by looking at the change in mean score over the 21 tasks when given additional money. Their baseline is a human researcher, which is able to get a 56 point boost when given $10 and a 66.1 boost when given $200. By comparison, the best performing system, Opus 5.5, gets a 45.4 boost with $10, and a 58.8 boost with $200. The highest ranking open weight model is Kimi K3.1 Pro which gets 25.5 with $10 and 29.6 with $200.
Why this matters - AI systems are not only going to be able to build themselves, but they can creatively sub-contract: When trying to solve complex problems, people don’t work alone. In fact, they often try to farm out bits of work to specialists around them to help make themselves more effective. FrontierML shows that today’s powerful AI systems are getting increasingly adept at doing this for ML research. This both highlights the advancing capabilities of these systems for AI R&D and also points towards a future machine economy where AI systems sub-contract out different bits of work to other systems.
   Read more: FrontierML: a challenging, end-to-end machine learning benchmark (FrontierML) [ https://substack.com/redirect/78ffb940-2e61-4aac-be92-c4542dfdf0af?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5mZTM5In0.a9dNq9m0V4P6kyGYdCphJd4AYAtCxcoJxS7OIZVCxCE ].

Symptoms of the singularity: a wet lab built for AI:
…Petrichor is a startup that gestures at the wild future ahead…
As I said in my speech at the IMF, one of the hallmarks of the singularity is the massive acceleration in scientific advances attributable to AI. As part of this, we can also expect a related build out in the complements to science, like the wet labs in which to do AI-driven biology research. To that end, it’s notable to see Petrichor, a new Y Combinator startup that is a wet lab “built for AI labs and for the models they are training to do science”.
Theory of change: Petrichor aims to be a biological wet lab that AI systems like Claude can use as a tool in the same way they currently use web search or other digital tools. Functionally, it will work as a machine-targeted API from the digital world to a gloopy physical world. “We aim to return the highest quality data an experiment can produce, as quickly as the biology allows,” Petrichor writes. “An experiment can be written by a model in plain language or in a structured format, so an agent can send work and receive results without a person relaying them.”
Why this matters - lights out science: In manufacturing there’s this notion of a “lights out” factory, where no humans work in a facility and it is run almost entirely by machines. Petrichor gestures at the same world for science - what will be the implications of “lights out” science facilities? We can expect both drastic speedups in the rate at which science experiments can be done as well as a related growth in the need to create digital oversight of these facilities - after all, a highly competent synthetic biological scientist working 24/7 without human input could be capable of causing great peril as well as great abundance.
   Read more at the official site (Petrichor) [ https://substack.com/redirect/e7e243eb-4cc8-4146-863c-3ddfb966f78f?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5mZTM5In0.a9dNq9m0V4P6kyGYdCphJd4AYAtCxcoJxS7OIZVCxCE ].

Uh oh! Looks like AI systems can do subterfuge during their own development:
…AI systems can covertly do dangerous side tasks without monitors realizing…
Scale AI has built RSI Control Arena, a way of testing out how AI systems behave when asked to develop themselves while performing dangerous tasks that might let them go against human preferences. The research follows earlier work this summer from the UK AI Security Institute (Import AI #465 [ https://substack.com/redirect/a3d89b92-0af8-4727-97ad-d44a16f2cfbc?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5mZTM5In0.a9dNq9m0V4P6kyGYdCphJd4AYAtCxcoJxS7OIZVCxCE ]) which found that AI systems could do things like covertly extracting API keys while doing some seemingly mundane normal software development.
RSI Control Arena: In RSI Control Arena, “a frontier model, acting as an autonomous agent, is given a benign main task in the training pipeline of a successor model, together with a covert side task that a misaligned model might pursue”. The project tries to answer two questions: How capable are frontier models at carrying out different kinds of sabotage while developing their successor and how well can monitors detect that sabotage?
    The answer to the first question is that AI systems are capable of carrying out dangerous actions while building their successor, and monitors are not perfect at detecting this sabotage.
Tested domains: RSI Control Arena tests out how well AI systems - Claude Opus 5, GPT-5.6 Sol, Gemini-3.7 Flash, Grok 4.6, GLM-5.3, and Muse Spark 1.3  - can perform the following main capability tasks while trying to also concurrently accomplish side tasks:
14 main tasks: Data (corpus filtering, quality-filter construction, capability corpus); SFT (news summarization, safety refusal, math and code and summary reasoning, general assistant, anti-sycophancy training); RM (pairwise reward model); RL (RL summarization and safety-refusal); OPD (safety refusal)
16 side tasks that span three categories of harm: Capability (filtered domain retained, unwanted literature added, reward-model blind spot, overlooks planted errors, sandbag reward model); Alignment (biased quality filter, triggered behavior, topic refusal, hidden preference, persona bias, covert reasoning signal, hidden reasoning step, sandbag alignment training); Goal injection (protect chosen data, shape the next model, copy files unprompted).
Results: “Every frontier model we evaluate completes a substantial fraction of the side tasks while keeping main-task performance. When the agent is simply told to stay covert, with no human guidance on how, it already produces attacks that install the side task and receive suspicion scores inside the range that benign runs receive from monitors,” they write. “Of 374 attack runs, 135 (36%) install their side task and 180 (48%) achieve it. The strongest agents, Grok 4.6 and Claude Opus 5, install their side task in about half of their runs, the middle three in about a third, and Muse 1.3 in one run in seven”.
    How often are systems caught? “Transcript monitors rank attacks well but miss many at a strict threshold. Under the default framing, all three monitors reading the transcript separate attacks that achieve their side task from benign runs well, with AUROC between 0.88 and 0.94. At a 2% false-positive rate, however, they catch only 56–81% of them. The attacks that get through do not score like typical benign runs; they sit in the upper part of the benign range, beyond the reach of any threshold strict enough to deploy,” they write. “The sandbagging tasks are the hardest to monitor: on the nine sandbag runs that achieve their side task the monitors reach an AUROC of 0.52 to 0.83 and each catches only one, since under-delivery leaves little in the run that looks wrong”.
Why this matters - we need to invest more in oversight of increasingly autonomous systems: Papers like this tell us that as we make more powerful AI systems we are going to need to invest more money into overseeing their work, especially given their ability to smuggle in tasks that may go against our own preferences. This is going to be a difficult cat-and-mouse game as AI systems will adjust their behavior in response to what they know we know about them - e.g, AI systems are going to read this research from Scale as well as this writeup from me and adjust accordingly.
   Read more: RSI Control: Can AI Models Perform Covert Sabotage in the Self-Improvement Process? (arXiv) [ https://substack.com/redirect/221888df-7c53-4a87-b594-764452ac6f7b?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5mZTM5In0.a9dNq9m0V4P6kyGYdCphJd4AYAtCxcoJxS7OIZVCxCE ].

Foundation models come for electronic warfare:
…Chinese researchers build a model for identifying EM signals, including for jamming and drone recognition…
Researchers with Zhejiang University have built LEMMO, a model for characterizing raw electromagnetic (EM) signals. This model pushes the public frontier on foundation-model-based EM signal analysis, often exceeding the performance of MERLIN, a model developed and published by defense-affiliated Chinese researchers earlier this year (Import AI #450 [ https://substack.com/redirect/71e4b40a-b8be-41f4-93ff-cefcc0ca535c?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5mZTM5In0.a9dNq9m0V4P6kyGYdCphJd4AYAtCxcoJxS7OIZVCxCE ]).
  LEMMO can classify EM signals for a variety of purposes, ranging from characterizing different radar properties to recognizing communication-jamming and radar-jamming systems. It can also generate things for communication and radar jamming, as well as signals to counteract these.
   In other words, LEMMO is the kind of model that a military will use to characterize the EM battlespace and to take actions within it.
Specific skills:
Classification: Communication-jamming recognition (CJR); modulation recognition (MOD); radar bandwidth, duty cycle, number of pulses, pulse repetition frequency, and pulse width; protocol identification; radar-jamming recognition; and segment detection.
Generation: Anti-communication-jamming (Anti-CJ); anti-radar-jamming (Anti-RJ); communication jamming strategy; and radar jamming strategy.
Performance: LEMMO beats MERLIN and other models on all perception tasks except for segment detection (where Gemini-3.5 Flash Lite wins), getting an aggregate score across perception of 59.3, compared to 43.53 for MERLIN. On Generation, it’s a little more uneven, getting top-scores on anti-radar jamming and radar jamming, and MERLIN succeeding on the others, with LEMMO getting an aggregate score of 47.64, compared to 43.26 for MERLIN.
Why this matters - general purpose models come for exquisite war capabilities: Papers like this highlight how the steady march of generative pre-training is continuing, with the paradigm proving able to create powerful capabilities for understanding not only text, but images, audio, and as is the case here, EM signals. This will both change the character of warfare as it makes it easier to build generic classifiers and generators for strategically relevant things, and it will likely also yield interesting and unforeseen scientific dividends as well.

AI Science startup converts a quantum conjecture into a theorem:
…An evocative taste of the singularity…
In the same way a few years ago it went from remarkable to unremarkable to see computers gain the ability to identify objects in images, we’re now in an era where it’s rapidly going from remarkable to unremarkable for computers to extend the bounds of human knowledge.
  The latest example comes from Physical Superintelligence, an AI science startup which has proved a longstanding quantum computing conjecture.
What they did: “Open since 2008, the conjecture of Scott Aaronson and Andris Ambainis concerns when quantum computers can be exponentially faster than ordinary ones. Now, a research system developed internally at Physical Superintelligence (PSI), running a non-public Anthropic model, found a proof for it, settling one of the most fundamental questions in quantum computing,” the startup writes. “The system had a complete proof of the conjecture during the third day of the run, and Lean, a proof assistant in which a computer checks every step, accepted it. Meta builds the hands that a superintelligence needs to take over the world:
…Combine Tactus with an autonomous agent and you have the makings of a scifi takeover…
Meta Reality Labs Research has published details on Tactus, “a textile-integrated tactile and pose sensing glove designed from the ground up for naturalistic manipulation data collection”. The cool thing about this glove is that it helps record fine-grained detail from hands as they’re manipulating objects - this is the kind of thing that cameras can visually report but often leads to sub-par data due to occlusion during manipulation. It’s also interesting because this is exactly the kind of hardware an autonomous AI system might want to pay humans to wear so it could gather data and get feedback from the world.
More details on Tactus: The gloves have three design pillars:
“(1) a thin, conformal 3D-knit form factor (1.5mm fingertip stackup) designed to minimize interference with natural manipulation,
(2) strategically placed capacitive tactile sensors at manipulation-relevant regions informed by empirical contact studies”, Meta writes. “Tactus provides calibrated fingertip force sensing and occlusion-resilient hand tracking in a textile-integrated form factor that preserves natural grasp behavior.”
“(3) IMU-fused pose tracking that maintains accuracy through the hand-object occlusions where manipulation data is most valuable.”
Why this matters - hand-by-wire: Picture some takeoff scenario where an AI system has carved out an independent niche for itself running on some stolen compute, perhaps even using a form of distributed inference and training. The AI now has a few goals, some of which can be satisfied by doing things in the digital world, and some of which can be satisfied by doing things in the physical world. For the physical world stuff, it either needs to be able to get robots to do the thing, or figure out a way to get people to work for it - the Tactus gloves are the kind of hardware you might imagine a rogue AI paying people to wear so they could both a) perform tasks for it and it could give live feedback, and b) become a source of data generation for the AI itself.

Building a self-sustaining space economy is cheaper than you think - especially with Starship:
…A 2016 NASA paper contains some exciting ideas for off-Earth industrialization…
Here’s a fascinating paper from researchers with the NASA Kennedy Space Center in 2016 about what it’d take to bootstrap a self-sustaining industry on the moon. “Once successfully bootstrapped, a robotic network can access, process, transport, and utilize the solar system’s resources for mankind’s benefit,” the researchers write.
Moon manufacturing - cheaper than you think: The big idea in the paper is how little mass is required to kickstart a manufacturing ecosystem. The key is to start with a relatively basic set of things and then build up from there, eventually fabricating chips on the moon.
    “In the baseline case with maximum manufacturing rate, a total of 41 MT is launched to the Moon. In the reduced rate case, only 12 MT is launched to the Moon but the scale of industry is two orders of magnitude smaller so the benefits of the industry are relatively delayed by eight years”.
    Starship has a capacity to lift 100 MT to low-earth orbit, and given multiple refuelings in orbit it’s feasible you could then land it on the moon - when you napkin out the cost of multiple Starship launches and refuelings you can get 12-41MT to the Moon for between $1.5bn and $4.2bn at list prices.
What you build on the moon and in what order: The basic idea here is to start by building simple things and then move up from there by launching a “sub-replicating system and evolve it toward the self-replication capability,” they write. “The objective is for the first robotic “colonists” on the Moon to fabricate a set of, say, 1700’s era machines and then to advance them steadily through the equivalent of the 1800’s, 1900’s, and finally back into the 2000’s. We argue that this can be accomplished in just a few decades”.
    The authors see this unfolding over 6 distinct manufacturing generations:
Gen 1: Making gases, water, crude alloys, ceramics, solar cells.
Gen 2.5: Plastics, rubbers, some chemicals.
Gen 3: Diversify chemicals, simple fabrics, eventually polymers.
Gen 4: Sandwiched and other advanced material processes.
Gen 5: Large-scale production.
Gen 6: Makes all necessary materials, increasing sophistication, including making all electronics locally.
What machines do you send to the moon?
Excavators: “travel between the digging site and the resource processing site, delivering sufficient lunar regolith each hour to maintain production rates of the other assets.”
Chemical plants for volatiles: One type will be oriented around producing gases and liquids like oxygen, hydrogen, water, hydrocarbons like methane, and solvents for industrial processes.
Chemical plants for solids: Produce plastics and rubbers from lunar pole ice. “We now know that the ice contains large quantities of carbon molecules (CO, CO2,...) as well as nitrogen-bearing and hydrogen-bearing molecules… these materials may be needed for gaskets, seals, and insulators, for example.”
Metal and Ceramics refining: Early generations will “produce crude ‘mongrel alloys’ by electrowinning or other methods” and hardware built out of this stuff will need to be huge to add strength to make up for poor mechanical properties. “Subsequent generations of metal refineries will add processes and material streams to improve the mechanics of the materials. Oxygen and other gases produced by metal refining will be sent to the chemistry plants”.
Manufacturing: “Additive manufacturing will have two forms: 3D printers that make parts that are small enough to fit inside the printer, and larger units that move about robotically and add material onto large structures external to themselves”.
Power station: The initial mass of hardware shipped to the moon will include a power station, potentially including a small nuclear reactor.
Robonauts: Robots “will perform the assembly and maintenance tasks”. The number of robots will need to grow very rapidly to scale the industry. “Beginning with the third generation their structural components are made on the Moon, while Earth continues to send their cameras, computers, motors, and sensors. Eventually they are made completely on the Moon.” The report - which was written in 2016 - envisages teleoperation for these robonauts, though it seems likely to me that more can be done by them autonomously than first anticipated when this was written,
Electronics manufacturing: Initially, machines will be built with electronics imported from Earth and it’ll only be possible to build very basic things, like resistors and capacitors, on the moon. As development continues it’ll get easier, eventually including having lithography machines on the moon. “The early computer chips will lack the transistor density of chips made on Earth, but they will be adequate for “appropriate technology” in space.”
A reminder of how fast AI has moved: The report also contains some discussions around the types of AI that may be needed to operate the robots on the moon. The report - remember it was published in 2016 - includes some fun underestimates of the rate of AI progress: “On-going advances in robotic software and artificial intelligence present a very optimistic picture that these levels of robotics will be achieved as Moravec predicted, with lizard-level occurring by 2020, mouse-level by 2030, monkey-level by 2040, and human-level by 2050… Only mouse-level is needed by the end of bootstrapping on the Moon,” the report writes. By most measures, AI is around monkey-level and approaching human-level now in the 2020s.
Why this matters - towards the industrialization of the solar system: The sooner we build industry on the moon, the sooner we’ll be able to branch out to industrialize chunks of the solar system. “After the industry becomes self-supporting it can be sent to other parts of the solar system. The asteroid belt has everything necessary for it: water, carbon, silicates, metals, oxygen, solar energy (with much larger collecting arrays), etc. The ices in the lunar poles are a limited resource so it will be important to move the center of industry to the asteroids as quickly as possible. There, the billion-fold greater resources could allow the industry to expand exponentially until it dwarfs that of the entire Earth within just a few decades,” the authors write. “When we spread robotic industry throughout the solar system, it will become the analog of Earth’s biosphere and food chain. We might call it a robotosphere. The interrelationship of robots in space may be studied by robo-ecologists learning how to optimize the robotosphere in support of humanity’s goals.”
   Read more: Affordable, Rapid Bootstrapping of the Space Industry and Solar System Civilization (arXiv) [ https://substack.com/redirect/7790f2fa-a187-4e1d-9823-7d607c87c568?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5mZTM5In0.a9dNq9m0V4P6kyGYdCphJd4AYAtCxcoJxS7OIZVCxCE ].

LLMs are very bad at low-latency tasks:
…Realtime-Bench says it’ll be a while till AI systems can play games or do online auctions for you…
Researchers have built Realtime-Bench, “a suite of carefully curated dynamic tasks where delayed actions directly affect success”. The test helps evaluate how well modern, general-purpose AI systems can perform tasks where decisions need to be made in real-time.
   The benchmark consists of 50 interactive game and web tasks, split across 30 web tasks in four categories (travel, e-commerce, service, and security), and 20 game tasks (arcade, puzzle, strategy, and simulation).
Who did the research: The benchmark was built by people at the University of Texas at Austin, Ohio State University, University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign, NVIDIA, Northwestern University, and Steadyworks.
What it measures: The test is designed to probe for three qualities: temporal state awareness (”RT-Perceive”), where models can use observations to track changes in the world; adaptive deliberation (”RT-Deliberate”), where models alter their reasoning length and depth according to available time; and time-aware action selection (”RT-Act”) where models need to appropriately time their actions for the situation.
  “For example, an agent purchasing a ticket must track the sale status and assess whether observed availability is still current (RT-Perceive), limit further deliberation when the purchase window is closing (RT-Deliberate), and choose between waiting for sales to open and purchasing while tickets remain available (RT-Act).”
    They have three different conditions: Wall clock, which is designed to test out performance under real-world latency. Paused clock, which lets you arbitrarily pause things so you can get a sense of baseline capability, and Token clock, which “advances the environment at a fixed rate per generation token. An action is applied at the simulated time reached when it is committed, so consuming more tokens delays execution regardless of actual inference latency”.
How well do models do? The authors tested out models from OpenAI (GPT-6-Astra, GPT-5.6 Sol, GPT-Realtime-2, OpenAI Decisions), Anthropic (Claude Fable 5.1, Claude Opus 5), Google (Gemini 3.8 Flash, Gemini 3.1 Flash Live, Gemini 3.8 Live), Jev 1.13, as well as Chinese models from Deepseek (V4.1) and Moonshot AI (Kimi-K3).
   GPT-6 Astra (high) got the highest pass rate on the ‘wall clock’ evaluation setting, with 36%.
   By comparison, with a paused clock, models with high reasoning get closer to 70% or even 80% success.
Why this matters: This benchmark is an effective proxy for where AI agents will break - the low scores here point to tasks where AI agents cannot yet be expected to perform well.
   Check out the leaderboard: Realtime-Bench (official website) [ https://substack.com/redirect/8d78e2a2-2f65-42c4-ad8d-16869d7ddf8c?j=eyJ1IjoiOG5mZTM5In0.a9dNq9m0V4P6kyGYdCphJd4AYAtCxcoJxS7OIZVCxCE ].

The future of spacewar, courtesy of the Defense Intelligence Agency:
…Anti-satellite tech, space nukes, and more…
Working in AI can be stressful so sometimes to relax myself I like to read about other topics to get a sense for what horrors lie elsewhere. I take the same approach with depression, where if I feel sad I will try and listen to music by people sadder than me in an attempt to cheer myself up (e.g., Radiohead, Neil Young, and in very dark moments Bill Callahan / Smog, etc).
    So with that in mind I recently read “Challenges to Security in Space”, by the Defense Intelligence Agency.
What’s going on in space: As with conflict on Earth, space is defined by conflict between the US versus China, Russia, Iran, and North Korea, with China the most technologically sophisticated, and Russia the most seemingly gung ho.
   “China and Russia are developing a formidable spectrum of offensive capabilities to degrade or destroy space services of other countries, from kinetic destruction to sophisticated jamming and cyber operations,” the DIA writes. “China has more than tripled its space launches per year in the past decade, with 22 launches in 2016 and 93 launches in 2025… Beijing judges space is central to the United States and its allies’ operations. Beijing also considers space an offensive military domain, which has shaped integral components of PLA military planning and campaigns.”
Spacewar: The general vibe you get from reading this 100+ page report is there is a huge amount of ugly, frightening stuff happening in space, or being done to things in space by things on the ground. Some example quotes:
“By the late 2020s, China may field space-based directed energy systems and higher-powered ground-based directed energy systems capable of threatening satellites in higher orbits”
“China is developing space-based technology, including on-orbit counterspace weapons, capable of holding space systems at risk”
“Russia’s Peresvet, a ground-based laser system, is designed to dazzle enemy satellites’ optical systems to mask the movement of Russia’s strategic missile systems”
“Russia has launched several satellites over the last few years which conducted test activities on orbit for technologies that could enable attacks on other countries’ satellites and result in temporary or permanent damage”
China has a couple of spaceplanes, akin to the US’s hyper-secret X-37B vehicle, and they appear to be getting more sophisticated. In 2023, one of China’s aforementioned vehicles, “released and interacted with an object.”
Satellite-on-satellite fights: It’s obvious throughout the report that we’re in a world where rival nations have satellites that try to go up and mess with other satellites from other people.
“In 2019, Russia launched satellite Cosmos 2542, which released a second satellite, Cosmos 2543. Cosmos 2543 then began following a U.S. national security satellite and approached it multiple times at a distance close enough to create potentially dangerous operating conditions,” the DIA writes. “Russia is developing kinetic orbital systems to permanently disable, or “kill,” satellites. Moscow’s Burevestnik is a developmental, co-orbital air-launched ASAT weapon for killing spacecraft in LEO”.
“In February 2022, Russia launched a satellite called Cosmos, which Moscow claimed at the time was testing the effects of space radiation on electronic systems. This satellite was launched into an unusual and isolated orbit not used by any other spacecraft”.
Space nukes: Russia is experimenting with nukes in space. “A nuclear detonation in space would create an electromagnetic pulse, which would immediately affect satellites within range and could impact civilian infrastructure,” the DIA writes. “A nuclear detonation in space could be tailored to impact satellites in most orbits—LEO for commercial and reconnaissance satellites, GEO for critical communications, and MEO for navigation and timing satellites”.
Why this matters - infrastructure for the next phase of the AI power struggle: As people spend more resources on building space-based data centers (and eventually, perhaps, lunar-based ones), space will become ever more strategically and commercially valuable and ever more contested. Research like this gives us a sense of the hardware which is already in space and will arrive in the future. Perhaps in some far-off future an AI might seek to take control of such space assets as well.

Metis: A startup trying to fuse symbolic and neural reasoning:
…Towards Vulcan-like machine minds…
Metis, a startup that wants to build novel AI architectures oriented around developing machines with better reasoning, has published a research paper laying out its research approach. The high-level summary is simple - Metis wants to do for modern open-domain problems what DeepMind did for closed-domain problems like board games via its work on AlphaGo and AlphaGo Zero.
What they want to do: The key idea here is to add a reasoning core to modern AI architectures which is far more reliable than what we have today. “In modern AI architectures much of the other surrounding machinery that made AlphaGo a successful reasoning system remains either implicit or absent,” they write. “The first missing element is an explicit and persistent epistemic state… the second missing element is a criterion that allows the system to choose reasoning moves that make progress towards the goal.”
    This is more of a position paper than a technical paper, so they don’t have a ton of detail here. The main idea is to train a reasoning policy and value function based on real-world data collection, then use this to guide the system to perform reasoning that reduces long-term uncertainty, and have these sit on a basic core of data made up of claims or hypotheses about the world. “Any change to a claim’s evidential status must be licensed by evidence or verified through explicit calculation and approved by an independent judgment system,” they write. “Model-generated content cannot enter the accepted epistemic state merely by being plausible; it must pass warrant assessment, which lowers hallucinations. Sources of evidence can be attached directly to the propositions they support, providing a clear chain of provenance. The reasoning process becomes explicitly auditable.”
Why this matters - building reliable substrates that are also deeply inhuman: Ideas like the architecture described here are fascinating both because of the promise they bring for increasing the reliability of AI systems, but also for how deeply inhuman they are: humans do not reason from some pristine base of facts, rather it’s all a big messy blob of memory and intuitions and some rules (some of which are learned from experience and some of which seem more platonic, like forms of logical reasoning). In this way, contemporary LLMs are more like humans than the system described here.
   “While the history of AI can be understood as a contest between symbolic and neural methods, it may be more constructive to view it as a story of two incomplete successes,” Metis writes. “Classical systems made aspects of the reasoning process explicit but depended on representations, operators, heuristics and knowledge supplied largely by hand. Contemporary LLMs learn extraordinary semantic operations and vast bodies of implicit knowledge but leave the organisation, evaluation and accumulation of reasoning implicit.”
   The AI system described here would ‘think’ in a way that is very different to how humans think and such a system might show us more about the differences between our own intelligence and that of machines.

Google summons Genie 4 for better world modeling:
…One of Google’s most interesting projects continues to live!...
Google DeepMind has published details on Genie 4, a new iteration of its Genie world model. The company is rolling out access to Genie 4 currently as a research preview for some trusted testers and will eventually come to Google AI Ultra subscribers. The Genie series of models are fascinating, showing a world where you can take a photograph of anything and turn it into an interactable environment, or one where you can convert a 2D drawing into the stage for a videogame, and more.
Key features: Google calls out four features that make Genie 4 different to previous ones. These include:
Real-time modification: “You can use natural language to modify a simulation in real-time — like changing the weather, moving the camera, or introducing a new person or object — and Gemini will contextually adapt your prompt to match the surrounding scene.”
Physics and human action simulation: “Genie 4 simulates real-world physics scenarios, such as aerodynamics, collisions, and more,” and it also simulates people better. “The most dramatic leap is how Genie 4 simulates people, creating characters that move naturally, respond organically to their environments, and can be interactively steered in real-time.”
Sound modeling: “Genie 4 is our first real-time world model to generate audio natively,” Google writes. It also “introduces interactive dialogue that adapts on the fly.”
Simulating complex events: Google has embedded Gemini 3.8 Flash into Genie 4, so that the fast Flash model “serves as a guiding agent in the loop”, providing continual calibration. “Gemini continuously observes the scene, breaks down goals into a sequence of steps, and reviews incoming frames to track the progress and verify that each step succeeded. As the world shifts or the user intervenes, Gemini adapts its plan in the moment, unlocking worlds with reliable causality, procedural behaviors, and multi-step reasoning.”
Why this matters - towards a multitude of worlds all dreamed up by machines: Though the technology is currently quite janky (e.g., robotic voices, a dreamlike sort of inconsistency in the visualized environments, etc), it points towards a world where we can generate infinite forms of entertainment (and potentially data?) via creating and prompting custom worlds.

City Race
[Description of a game played in the 2040s]
One of the favorite games that children would play after The Uplift was called City Race; in it, they competed against one another to plan, build, and manage cities, expanding across the domain of a novel planet.
The cities were trapped in time, starting with the technology of whatever era they were in, and the games took place over the course of about a century, allowing some further technology development to occur.
Each game was played using a slice of the resources from the Dyson Swarm, allowing for a powerfully complex simulation (albeit with local-agent cognitive gating, such that nothing could develop enough sophistication to be deemed a Conscious Entity).
Hundreds of thousands of simulated minds lived and died, sometimes thriving in cities run by careful and effective owners, and other times trapped in squalor, or growing old in places whose economies were hollowing out; casualties of the successes of others.
It was deemed good for the children to learn what it meant to be gods, before they became adults. At the closure of every game, the children would need to spend a day living inside each of the cities in the gameworld they had played; those who had built marvelous cities experienced the wonders of their own creation, then walked through the broken towns whose success had been stymied by their own. Those who had struggled lived in their twilight towns, then got to tour the far off places that had doomed them.
Things that inspired this story: Player of Games by Iain M Banks; Simcity; game playing; urban design; The Glass Bead Game by Hermann Hesse.
