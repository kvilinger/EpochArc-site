The European Union's executive arm has confirmed it was targeted by an “AI-generated” cyberattack by an unknown individual or group. European Commission spokesperson Thomas Regnier ⁠said on Thursday: "I can confirm that the commission has recently taken necessary ​steps in response to a detected cybercampaign.” "As a protective and preventive measure, we temporarily took down several ​applications and most of them have since been restored," he continued. "The investigation is still ongoing. We ​are taking any necessary steps, based on the findings as they come." Regnier said that the “AI-generated cybercampaign” was detected on October 22. The commission oversees areas including trade, immigration, technology and aspects of European defense and security policy. The announcement comes amid an uptick in unusual cyber events, outages and alleged attacks. “Individual systems were targeted and, in line with our standard practice, we have informed those concerned,” a European Commission spokesperson told Newsweek. “While AI can help detect vulnerabilities and prevent cyberattacks, it can also be misused for cyber operations,” they said. “The commission has been working to strengthen the security of its systems and continues to take appropriate measures. “The investigation is still ongoing. We are taking any necessary steps based on the findings." Unanswered Questions in European Union Attack Regnier did not state whether the EC had identified any suspects, but said that there was so far no evidence of a ransomware motivation or a “data breach affecting commission staff as a whole.” He said that the campaign utilized an AI tool that was available to the public, without specifying which one. Earlier on Thursday, Politico reported that personal data might have been compromised by an attack on the Commission. “The commission believes it has contained most of the attack, but the full extent of any damage remains unclear,” an anonymous EU official told the outlet. “The systems may be coming back online. The answers are taking a little longer.” Disruptions reportedly lasted over 10 days and impacted the EC’s HR, planning and payroll processing platforms. On November 5, CERT-EU—the organization’s cybersecurity monitor—said it detected cyber campaigns worldwide using ARTEX, an AI-powered penetration-testing tool. “We assess that ARTEX’s public availability will likely accelerate adoption by threat actors, resulting in more frequent, faster, and more scalable automated attacks with a higher success rate,” it wrote in a blog on November 5. EC Attack Adds to Hacker Concerns The attack that the commission confirmed on Thursday is only the latest in a series of high-profile cyber events. The announcement coincides with a hacker group publishing personal details of thousands of JPMorgan Chase customers. "Based on our investigation so far, we believe the attackers accessed certain marketing data held by those vendors, which may include some customer names and email addresses," a spokesperson for the bank said. Malicious code has recently been planted on the websites of some of the world’s largest brands, including the BBC, CNN and HSBC. Researchers say that hackers used scripts hosted by Optimizely, a firm providing software allowing businesses to test different versions of websites. In an update this week, Optimizely said that "an unauthorized party exploited a vulnerability” in its digital testing platform, but that it had “fixed the vulnerability and removed the injected code from affected projects.” This came on the heels of a global Microsoft outage on November 12 and 13 and a rare nationwide groundstop being issued by the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA). Microsoft later attributed it to a software defect rather than a cyberattack, while federal officials said the FAA outage and resulting groundings were due to copper-to-fiber network upgrades in Arkansas or Texas that overwhelmed air traffic communications networks across the country with excessive data. But taken together, the recent incidents have sparked concern over the safety and reliability of digital systems which support political and economic infrastructure in the U.S. and worldwide. “There has been a noticeable uptick in inquiries regarding bunkers and disaster preparedness,” Michael Brait, CEO of the Massachusetts-based Bunker Building Consulting LLC, previously told Newsweek. “People are concerned with security and the stockpiling of food, water and supplies,” he said. But some, including President Donald Trump, have nevertheless said that there is no cause for alarm. “Tell them to relax, everything is good. These are individual events,” the president said on Fox News when asked to respond to reports of some Americans stockpiling food and water.
