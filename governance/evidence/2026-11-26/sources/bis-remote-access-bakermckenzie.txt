BIS Restricts Remote Access to Advanced AI Chips and Formally Rescinds the AI Diffusion Rule
November 18, 2026
By Alison J. Stafford Powell (US), Janet K. Kim (US), Lise S. Test (US) and Rob O'Brien (US)
14 Mins Read
Share

On November 16, 2026, the US Department of Commerce’s Bureau of Industry and Security (“BIS“) released an interim final rule (“IFR“) amending the Export Administration Regulations (“EAR“) to formally rescind the AI Diffusion Rule; impose a new destination-based license requirement for exports, reexports, or in-country transfers of advanced computing items to five additional countries; impose a new end use-based license requirement for advanced computing items destined for non-US data centers that provide remote access to a “significant volume of compute power” to end users headquartered in Macau or a Country Group D:5 destination (including China); and cut off the possibility of overcoming certain presumptions for “front-end fabricators” and OSAT companies where the integrated circuit designer is located in, or has an ultimate parent company headquartered in, a Country Group D:1, D:4, or D:5 destination or Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand, or Brazil. The BIS press release is available here.
The IFR is scheduled to be published in the Federal Register on November 19, 2026 and takes effect on publication, with no delayed compliance date.  While the IFR does grandfather certain shipments already en route aboard a carrier pursuant to actual orders as of the publication date, this 30-day savings clause may have limited practical effect where the shipment’s end use also triggers one of the new license requirements that applies on an ongoing basis, for conduct subsequent to the shipment.   The IFR also provides a 90-day period for certain existing data center arrangements to be licensed by BIS, discussed below. Comments are due 60 days after publication.
The IFR is of most immediate interest to manufacturers, resellers, exporters, and reexporters of advanced AI chips, GPUs, and servers containing the foregoing; operators of data centers outside the United States, including US-headquartered cloud providers and the parties that support them, including co-location providers and financing parties; and “front-end fabricators” and outsourced semiconductor assembly and test (“OSAT“) companies. Citing Executive Order 14434, BIS uses the term “Super Intelligence” in place of “Artificial Intelligence” (“AI“) throughout the IFR; this post uses “AI.”
The key takeaways of the IFR are the following, which we address below in turn:
Rescission of the AI Diffusion Rule
New destination-based license requirement
New Remote Access End Use Controls
Continuing Jurisdiction over ECCN 3A090 Chips in Foreign-Made Servers
Amendment to ECCN 3A090 Front-End Fabricator and OSAT Presumptions
The IFR refers multiple times to companies that are “headquartered,” or have an “ultimate parent company” headquartered in, certain countries, including the United States, Macau, and Country Group D:5 destinations.  The quoted terms are used in other provisions of the EAR and are not defined.  It remains unclear whether BIS will adopt more functional interpretations (looking to substance over form, as some sister agencies have in their national security-based programs) and also whether it is possible for a company (such as a joint venture) to have more than one “ultimate parent company,” e.g., a Singapore company in which a US company holds a 60% interest and a Chinese company holds the remaining 40%.
1. Rescission of AI Diffusion Rule
The IFR formalizes the rescission of the AI Diffusion Rule, which BIS announced in May 2025 along with its intent not to enforce the rule. (See our previous blog post here.) The AI Diffusion Rule was issued in the last days of the Biden Administration on January 13, 2025 and introduced a worldwide license requirement and complex export controls on certain advanced computing integrated circuits and on model weights for certain advanced closed-weight AI models.
The IFR removes ECCN 4E091 and its controls on model weights and the related foreign direct product rule in § 734.9(l).  The IFR also removes License Exceptions AIA and LPP, the country tiers created by the AI Diffusion Rule, and the Data Center Validated End-User Authorization. License Exception ACM is retained, as are pre-existing License Exceptions NAC and ACA.
2. New Destination-Based License Requirement
Amended § 742.6(a)(6)(iii)(A)(1) requires a license to export, reexport, or transfer (in-country) advanced computing items specified in ECCNs 3A090 (except 3A090.c), 4A090, and the related .z paragraphs, together with associated software and technology, to or within Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand, and Brazil, which BIS identifies as presenting a higher-than-normal risk of diversion.
The license requirement does not apply where the ultimate consignee or end user is headquartered in, or has an ultimate parent company headquartered in, the United States, provided the exporter, reexporter, or transferor files monthly reports under new § 743.10, due no later than the first business day of the month following the transaction. License applications will generally be reviewed on a case-by-case basis, taking into account the effectiveness of the destination’s export control regime and its coordination with BIS on enforcement.
A license remains required for advanced computing items destined for countries in Country Groups D:1, D:4, and D:5 (excluding those also in Country Groups A:5 or A:6) and for the United Arab Emirates (“UAE“) unless the ultimate consignee and all end users are approved UAE entities listed in supplement no. 8 to part 740. (See our previous blog post here.)
3. New Remote Access End Use/End User Controls
The IFR focuses heavily on closing perceived loopholes around remote access to US compute power.  As we previously reported, BIS issued guidance on May 31, 2026 confirming that a license was required for advanced computing items destined for use by entities headquartered in, or whose ultimate parent company is headquartered in, Country Group D:5 or Macau. BIS describes the new data center remote access control as an extension of that existing end user/end use requirement to remote access. The IFR does not change the definitions of export, reexport, or transfer (in-country) to include pure remote access, which is what the proposed Remote Access Security Act bill seeks to accomplish. Rather, BIS leverages its existing authority to regulate the export, reexport, and in-country transfer of advanced computing items and imposes license requirements based on the intended end use of those items, including its authority to impose additional license requirements for subsequent changes in the end use or end user of those items.
a. Data Center Volume-Based End Use Control in New § 744.24
New § 744.24 requires a license to export, reexport, or transfer (in-country) advanced computing items where there is “knowledge” that the item is destined to an overseas data center that is providing or will provide access to more than 12,000,000 “aggregate TPP” to or for companies headquartered in, or whose ultimate parent company is headquartered in, Macau or a Country Group D:5 destination. “Aggregate TPP” is the sum of the total processing performance (“TPP“) of the covered integrated circuits or commodities in a group. The threshold can be met at a single data center or across “linked data centers,” which include digitally linked as well as physically linked facilities.
Commercial recommender systems. The restrictions do not apply where compute power is provided only for a “commercial recommender system,” defined as an AI tool exclusively designed and used to rank, filter, or suggest commercial products, services, or content, based on user behavior, transaction history, or item attributes, such as those used by online retailers or social media companies. Systems designed or used to generate novel content in response to user inputs are excluded.  Before relying on this exception, providers must report certain information to BIS identifying the customer, the TPP provided, and the due diligence performed.
Indirect access. The preamble emphasizes the broad reach of the new controls by stating that they even reach a data center providing remote access to a non-Country Group D:5 customer that is in turn training AI models for, participating in the training of AI models for, or providing AI models to, a Country Group D:5 company, e.g., providing remote access to a Canadian customer training AI models for a Chinese company.
Due diligence and “knowledge.”  Unlike the more conventional “knowledge” standard in the EAR, which generally turns on the information available to a party and its appropriate response to red flags, the IFR imposes an affirmative due diligence obligation to make specified inquiries even in the absence of red flags.  The failure to do so could result in “knowledge” of facts that trigger license requirements.  Accordingly, suppliers are expected to obtain from the foreign data center, and be able to provide, information on its know-your-customer checks, including whether any customer is subject to direct or indirect ownership or control by persons located or headquartered in, or whose ultimate parent company is headquartered in, Macau or Country Group D:5. A data center that declines or is unable to provide this information presents a red flag that presumptively establishes “knowledge” unless resolved before the transaction proceeds.
No De Minimis. Reexporters and transferors may not rely on the EAR’s de minimis provisions where there is “knowledge” that a US-origin covered item incorporated into a foreign-made item will be used in the restricted end use.  This represents a departure from the EAR’s traditional de minimis rules (including the secondary incorporation principle) and continues with the “see-through” rule that BIS has deployed in other sensitive areas, such as the foreign direct product rule, where parties cannot rely on incorporation principles to overcome “knowledge” that an item is ultimately intended for a prohibited end use.
US-headquartered operators. The “significant volume” end use licensing trigger in § 744.24 contains no exclusion for US-headquartered companies (which is only available for the new five-destination license requirement, discussed above), so a US cloud provider’s data center outside the United States that serves Macau- or Country Group D:5-headquartered customers above the threshold appears to be within scope.
Later changes in end use and notification requirement. The control is not limited to the original shipment. It applies regardless of whether the person in possession of the item was a party to the original transaction, and a change from a non-prohibited to a prohibited end use is a transfer (in-country) requiring authorization. Parties (e.g., OEMs or resellers) that ship without a license because they lack “knowledge” must notify the recipient that BIS authorization is required before the items are used for the restricted end use and that the know-your-customer guidance in supplement no. 3 to part 732 must be followed.
90-day license requirement for existing arrangements. Notably, data centers that were already providing a “significant volume” of compute power to Macau- or Country Group D:5-headquartered companies before publication of the IFR must obtain a license within 90 days of the effective date to continue doing so “or face future export restrictions.” The provision does not refer to the 12,000,000 aggregate TPP threshold, although the preamble uses “significant volume” to describe the § 744.24(a)(1) control, which suggests that the same threshold applies. The IFR does not describe what the future export restrictions would be or explain the consequences if an application is filed within 90 days but no response is received from BIS.
Licensing policy. Applications under § 744.24 will be reviewed on a case-by-case basis.
b. Existing End Use Control in § 744.23
The 12,000,000 aggregate TPP threshold is not a safe harbor. As a conforming change to implement the rescission of the AI Diffusion Rule, the IFR returns to § 744.23(a)(3)(i)(A), the pre-existing end use-based license requirement for advanced computing items destined “for an entity” headquartered in, or with an ultimate parent company headquartered in, Macau or Country Group D:5. BIS states that this end use control “is not, and has never been limited only to” shipments to such entities as ultimate consignees or end users, and that it extends to transactions on their behalf, for example where an entity is the purchaser, directly or indirectly, or is “the exclusive or primary (greater than 50 percent of total compute) remote end user of the data center computing capacity.” A data center arrangement below the § 744.24 threshold can therefore still require a license under § 744.23.
BIS encourages exporters, reexporters, and transferors to consider reviewing past and current shipments to data centers outside the United States that have Macau- or Country Group D:5-headquartered remote users for compliance with the end use controls in §§ 744.23 and 744.24 and notes that any “entities in violation of this requirement should submit a voluntary self-disclosure (see Section 764.5 of the EAR).” The earlier May 31, 2026 BIS guidance clarifying the continued existence of the license requirement for advanced computing items to entities located in third countries but headquartered in, or whose ultimate parent company is headquartered in, Macau or Country Group D:5, stated that bona fide data center operators were not required to cease the ongoing use, storage, disposal, or servicing of advanced computing items because of that guidance “until further notice from BIS.” The IFR does not expressly say whether it constitutes that notice, nor is it clear whether the voluntary self-disclosure comment is only directed to activities that occurred after the BIS guidance of May 31, 2026 or some other date, such as the effective date of the IFR.
The preamble of the IFR also imposes a license requirement, purportedly under § 744.23(a)(3)(i)(A), on “bona fide operators of data centers who are otherwise engaged in activities consistent with the EAR and were providing remote access used for ‘commercial recommender systems’ (as defined in the new § 744.24(d)(1)).”  Such operators “may continue providing these services for 90 days, but must obtain a license following expiration of this period to continue these services.”  It is not clear from the IFR why “commercial recommender systems” are excluded from controls (and therefore licensing) under § 744.24 as “not pos[ing] the same national security threat” but are subject to licensing under § 744.23.  In addition, the 90-day license requirement is not reflected in § 744.23 itself and only appears in the preamble.  By contrast, the 90-day license requirement under § 744.24 appears in both the preamble and the body of § 744.24.
4. Continuing Jurisdiction over ECCN 3A090 Chips in Foreign-Made Servers
BIS retains the worldwide scope of the advanced computing foreign direct product rule in § 734.9(h) and, responding to questions it has received, describes that provision’s “expansive application.” According to BIS, where a foreign-produced item is subject to the EAR under § 734.9(h), BIS retains jurisdiction over that item after it is incorporated into another foreign-produced item. “Knowledge” about the intended end use or destination for the foreign-produced item is not a relevant factor in this analysis.  BIS gives the example of an ECCN 3A090 integrated circuit incorporated into a foreign-produced GPU board or server, where BIS “continues to have jurisdiction over the IC and license requirements may apply to the export, reexport, or transfer (in-country) of the IC (and by extension, the server).” BIS acknowledges that it generally does not retain jurisdiction over items after incorporation into other items, absent the de minimis provisions, but distinguishes ECCN 4A090 and the .z paragraphs on the basis that items meet those entries only if they contain an ECCN 3A090 integrated circuit. Accordingly, “even third parties who only receive a 4A090 item are on notice that such items contain an ECCN 3A090 IC.”
5. Amendment to ECCN 3A090 Front-End Fabricator and OSAT Presumptions
Under pre-existing Note 1 to ECCN 3A090.a, “front-end fabricators” and OSAT companies must presume that an “applicable advanced logic integrated circuit” is specified in ECCN 3A090.a and designed or marketed for data centers and, unless one of three conditions for overcoming this presumption is met, must comply with the license requirements applicable to 3A090.a items. The IFR makes two of these three pathways for overcoming that presumption (self-attestations by “front-end fabricators” and OSATs) unavailable where the IC designer is located in, or has an ultimate parent company headquartered in, a Country Group D:1, D:4, or D:5 destination or Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand, or Brazil.
***
Overall, this IFR raises a number of unresolved questions, including: who has responsibility for seeking licenses where multiple parties have knowledge that BIS licenses are required; the consequences if BIS does not adjudicate license applications within 90 days; what types of services constitute the provision of a “significant volume” of compute power (in particular, as to PaaS); how to interpret “ultimate parent company” in the context of entities with multiple ultimate beneficial owners; how to interpret “headquarters” when a company is incorporated in one country but has its center of management in a different country; and the implications for parties financing large data center projects or purchases, which typically take a security interest in the underlying purchases. Impacted companies are encouraged to assess these questions and submit comments within the 60-day comment period.
We will continue to monitor and update on any further developments on these topics, including the progress of the Remote Access Security Act.
