Abstract
This document describes how a service operator binds usage
   preferences (e.g., "no AI training") to paths by writing ordinary
   robots.txt groups keyed by a per-purpose policy token in the User-
   Agent line.  It introduces no new directive names and no new grammar:
   the path language, longest-match precedence, and group structure of
   robots.txt are not changed.

1.  Introduction

   A service operator binds usage preferences (e.g., "no AI training")
   to paths by writing ordinary robots.txt groups keyed by a per-purpose
   *policy token* in the User-Agent line.

   User-Agent: __usage_ai-training
   Disallow: /
   Allow: /press-kit/

                       Figure 1: A policy-token group
Kelley                     Expires 3 May 2027                   [Page 2]
Internet-Draft         Training Preference Signal           October 2026
This expresses a preference _against_ AI training for all paths
   except those under /press-kit/, for which it expresses a preference
   _in favor_ (Section 4.2).

   This document introduces no new directive names and no new grammar;
   the path language, longest-match precedence, and group structure
   defined by [ROBOTS] are not changed.  As a result, the wide variety
   of existing parsers deployed across the Internet will continue to
   interpret traffic management signals as they did previously, and will
   ignore policy-token groups as they would any group for a user agent
   they do not recognize.

1.1.  Goals

   1.  *Path-specific bindings.* Preferences can be expressed per path,
       and not only site-wide (Section 4.2).

   2.  *Minimal distance from RFC 9309.* Semantics, and ideally syntax,
       stay as close to [ROBOTS] as possible, so that authors and
       implementers can use the mental model they already have.

   3.  *No large-scale duplication.* robots.txt files are size-
       constrained (Section 2.5 of [ROBOTS]), and some deployed files
       already contain heavily duplicated groups.  Expressing
       preferences does not require restating existing rules
       (Section 4.1).

   4.  *Unchanged legacy behavior.* A parser or consumer that does not
       implement this mechanism behaves as before.  This holds for
       parsers that are actually deployed, not only for idealized RFC
       9309 parsers.  Conversely, implementations treat files that do
       not use this mechanism exactly as before (Section 4.2 and
       Section 4.3).

   5.  *Consistent interpretation across consumers.* This mechanism does
       not depend on the divergent parts of deployed parser behavior
       (Section 4.1).

2.  Conventions and Definitions

   The key words "MUST", "MUST NOT", "REQUIRED", "SHALL", "SHALL NOT",
   "SHOULD", "SHOULD NOT", "RECOMMENDED", "NOT RECOMMENDED", "MAY", and
   "OPTIONAL" in this document are to be interpreted as described in
   BCP 14 [RFC2119] [RFC8174] when, and only when, they appear in all
   capitals, as shown here.

   service operator:  An entity that operates an HTTP service, such as a
Kelley                     Expires 3 May 2027                   [Page 3]
Internet-Draft         Training Preference Signal           October 2026
website, and publishes a robots.txt file for it.  For preferences
      expressed through this mechanism, the service operator is the
      "declaring party" (Section 2 of [VOCAB]).  [ROBOTS] calls this
      party a "service owner".

   consumer:  An entity that interprets preference signals that a
      service operator expresses through the mechanism defined in this
      document.

   parser:  In the sense of [ROBOTS]; software that parses robots.txt
      files and matches product tokens and paths against their rules.

   policy token:  A product token (Section 2.2.1 of [ROBOTS]) whose RFC
      9309 signals a consumer uses to determine how content will be
      used.  A policy token need not appear in the User-Agent header
      field (Section 10.1.5 of [HTTP]) of any outbound request.  This
      document introduces a new, cross-vendor policy token
      (Section 4.1).

   proprietary policy token:  A policy token documented by a single
      consumer.  Some service operators already include these in their
      robots.txt files.

3.  Vocabulary

   The __usage_ai-training policy token corresponds to the definition
   that the IETF aipref Working Group has arrived at (Section 4.1 of
   [VOCAB]).  The following definitions are quoted from that document:

   AI Training:  Using an asset to modify the learned parameters of a
      generative AI model.

      The training of models that are used to perform exclusively non-
      generative tasks is not included in this category, even if the
      model is capable of generative tasks.

   Generative AI model:  An AI model that is used to generate synthetic
      content in one or more modalities.  Alternatively, an AI model
      that is made available for use to generate synthetic content in
      one or more modalities.  This definition does not include a model
      that is used, or made available for use, for classification,
      ranking, or scoring, even where the model generates rationale for
      its output.

   This document applies those definitions as follows:

   asset:  The content retrieved from a path.  The preferences that
Kelley                     Expires 3 May 2027                   [Page 4]
Internet-Draft         Training Preference Signal           October 2026
apply to an asset are those expressed in the robots.txt file in
      effect when the asset was retrieved (Section 2.4 of [ROBOTS]; see
      also [ATTACH]).

   This document does not define a parallel vocabulary; it tracks the
   work of the aipref Working Group.  Implementers SHOULD consider
   updating their definitions, and adding or removing vocabulary terms,
   as the aipref Working Group reaches consensus in additional areas.

4.  Design

4.1.  The Policy Token

   This document defines the policy token __usage_ai-training.

   The policy token conforms to the product-token syntax in
   Section 2.2.1 of [ROBOTS] (letters, _, -).

   Consumers MUST match policy tokens exactly, apart from case, as for
   any product token.  (Note that some deployed parsers match more
   flexibly; the __ prefix makes accidental matches unlikely.)

   A single group MAY name several policy tokens (consecutive User-Agent
   lines; Section 2.2 of [ROBOTS]) to bind its rules to several
   purposes.  Policy tokens and other product tokens MAY be mixed in a
   group.

4.2.  Checking for Preferences

   To determine whether a preference has been expressed for a given
   path, a consumer looks up the path and policy-token combination as
   specified in Sections 2.2.1 and 2.2.2 of [ROBOTS].

   However, wildcard groups (i.e., User-Agent: *) MUST NOT be
   interpreted as matching the policy token.

   If the lookup finds that the path is disallowed, a preference against
   the use described by the policy token has been expressed for that
   path.  If it finds that the path is allowed, a preference in favor of
   that use has been expressed.

   If no group contains the __usage_ai-training policy token (i.e.,
   there is no User-Agent: __usage_ai-training line in the file), then
   no preference has been expressed.  If such a file contains a
   proprietary policy token, consumers SHOULD interpret that token as
   they would in the absence of this document, but MUST NOT treat it as
   expressing a preference in the context of this document (see
   Section 4.5).

4.3.  Independence from Crawler Rules

   Consumers MUST interpret the RFC 9309 signals being expressed based
   on their own product token(s) exactly as they would in the absence of
   this document.  That is, ExampleBot still checks whether there are
   rules for ExampleBot; if there are, they still express the service
   operator's preference about crawling.  Nothing in this document
   changes that signaling.

   Content-usage preferences and traffic management signals can both be
   expressed in robots.txt, but they are independent and orthogonal.

4.4.  Effects on Crawling Behavior

   This policy token is not a directive for crawling behavior.

   A consumer MAY use preferences to inform crawling.  For example, if
   it crawls only for purposes covered by the policy token, it MAY
   refrain from retrieving resources for which a preference against
   those purposes has been expressed.

   If a service operator wishes to limit crawling, it MAY use groups for
   crawlers' product tokens in robots.txt to do so.

4.5.  Precedence

   Specific expressions SHOULD take precedence over general ones.

   Consumers MAY document that they interpret rules that match
   proprietary policy tokens as being more specific than the __usage_ai-
   training policy token.

   Private agreements MAY override preferences expressed via this
   mechanism.

5.  Security Considerations

   The security considerations of Section 3 of [ROBOTS] apply to this
   mechanism.

6.  Privacy Considerations

   This document does not introduce any new privacy considerations.

7.  IANA Considerations

   This document has no IANA actions.
