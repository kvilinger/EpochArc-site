United Airlines Alone In Blocking AI Agents From Booking Flights—Rivals Say Customers Assume Risk

By
Suzanne Rowan Kelleher
,

Staff Writer.

Suzanne Rowan Kelleher covers travel for Forbes.

Follow Author

Nov 12, 2026, 09:42am EST

Topline

United Airlines on Wednesday announced it would restrict third-party agentic AI bots and agents’ access to its ⁠digital platforms, citing data security for sensitive passenger information and its loyalty program—while competitors say risk belongs to customers who let a bot plan and book their trips.

United Airlines is the only major US carrier to pump the brakes on working with third-party agentic AI platforms, citing security concerns. (Photo Illustration by Budrul Chukrut)

SOPA Images/LightRocket via Getty Images

Key Facts

“Sharing account credentials with personal, third-party, AI assistants can expose sensitive information” which “may result in unintended purchases, account changes, or use of MileagePlus miles,” United said in a statement shared with Forbes.

Other major U.S. airlines—Delta, American, JetBlue—allow customers to book flights with agentic AI platforms but say the customer ultimately assumes any risk.

In recent years, loyalty rewards accounts have been a particularly lucrative
target of cyberhackers
, while AI agents are increasingly able to book travel on passengers’ behalf, such as Meta’s
Muse
and SpaceXAI’s Grok Bot.

Large online travel booking platforms such Expedia, Booking and TripAdvisor have been working with ChatGPT-parent OpenAI
since last year
.

European budget airline easyJet integrates flight booking and live flight-status tracking directly into the ChatGPT mobile interface, the carrier’s CEO Kenton Jarvis
announced on LinkedIn
last month.

Crucial Quote

“This is the wild west, with everybody out there trying to figure out how to outgun the other guy,” John Gradek, who teaches aviation risk management at McGill University, told Forbes. “It’s going to take one or two major incidents to slow the horses down on this one. Will it happen? It’s not a question of if; it’s a question of when.”

Other US Airlines Say Customers Assume Risk If They Book With AI Tools

“We’re seeing an explosion of AI chatbots and agentic AI in the hands of consumers,” Gradek told Forbes. “Other than United, other major U.S. airlines are saying ‘we’ll put up as much of a defense as we possibly can, but if the customer wants to use it, let the customer take the consequences.’” Delta Air Lines allows AI assistants to complete a booking on behalf of a customer if it can “appropriately authenticate using a customer’s Delta login credentials (SkyMiles number and password),” a spokesperson for the airline told Forbes, noting that “customers are responsible for overseeing actions and authorizing any transactions taken by AI assistants on their behalf.” American Airlines “continues to evaluate” how it will work with AI companies, the carrier told Forbes in a statement, noting “some AI tools are able to facilitate the purchase of American Airlines tickets” while insisting that “protecting our customers and their experience remains our priority.” On Wednesday, JetBlue President Marty St. George told attendees at an industry conference his airline “should be responsive to what customers want” and “if customers really think that’s a better way to do it, we need to figure out how to meet them where they are.” Meanwhile, “United understandably wants to control the conversation with customers and is afraid it’ll be machine-to-machine talking rather than machine-to-person talking,” Gradek said.

Airline Loyalty Programs Are A Top Concern

“Loyalty points are a sort of currency, and airlines have to model themselves against how banks are dealing with agentic AIs,” Gradek told Forbes. “Banks have got firewalls coming out the wazoo. You cannot give your bank account information to an AI bot and have the bot log into your account. You need two-factor identification, and that’s a big part of how the banking industry is keeping transactions secure from AI bots.” United’s statement specifically mentioned its loyalty program as a top concern. “Airlines are concerned about AI bots accessing frequent flyer programs and using that as a back door into the system, and go in and clean out customers’ accounts,” Gradek said. “It is a valid concern, but welcome to 2026.”

Key Background

United’s statement noted “corporate leaders in the banking and retail industries have taken similar actions” and encouraged “others to consider measures that preserve the data and security of their customers and strengthen safety.” It also warned that “automated AI actions have the potential to violate FAA and DOT regulations,” giving the example of flight check-ins that require human acknowledgement that luggage does not contain dangerous items such as explosives. “United has a valid point there,” Gradek told Forbes. “You need a human who’s responsible for that bag to vouch for it. Letting an AI agent do that would be in violation of regulations.”

Tangent

MCR Hotel CEO Tyler Morse wants to ban AI booking agents because they are more rational shoppers and resist the upsells that tempt human guests,
Skift reported
.
